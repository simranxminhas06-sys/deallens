# Whole Foods Market, Inc. — Company Overview (Simplified Demo Document, FY2016 basis)

_This is a simplified, synthetic summary prepared for the DealLens demo. Figures are
rounded approximations of publicly reported results and should not be treated as an
exact reproduction of any filing._

## Section 1: Business Description

Whole Foods Market, Inc. operates natural and organic food supermarkets across the
United States, Canada, and the United Kingdom. The company built its brand around
strict quality standards, prohibited-ingredients lists, and a reputation as the
category leader in natural and organic grocery. By fiscal 2016, the natural/organic
category had become increasingly contested as conventional grocers (Kroger, Costco)
and Amazon itself (via AmazonFresh) expanded organic and private-label assortments
at lower price points, pressuring Whole Foods' historical premium positioning —
a dynamic the company and outside commentators referred to publicly as "Whole
Paycheck" perception risk.

## Section 2: Financial Highlights (Fiscal Year 2016, ended September 25, 2016)

- Total revenue: approximately $15.7 billion.
- Store count: approximately 431 stores at fiscal year-end, concentrated in
  affluent urban and suburban markets across the U.S., with a smaller footprint
  in Canada and the U.K.
- Comparable store sales: declined approximately 2.5% for the fiscal year,
  marking multiple consecutive quarters of comparable-sales softness.
- Operating income: approximately $675 million (operating margin of roughly
  4.3%), down from prior-year levels amid promotional activity and cost
  pressure.
- Employees: approximately 87,000 full-time and part-time employees ("Team
  Members," in the company's terminology).

## Section 3: Strategic Context Disclosed to Investors

- Management publicly announced a cost-reduction program targeting
  approximately $300 million in annualized savings through supply chain and
  administrative efficiency, alongside a "365 by Whole Foods Market" smaller-
  format, lower-price store concept intended to reach price-sensitive
  customers.
- The company faced pressure from an activist investor (Jana Partners) that had
  taken a stake and was pushing for strategic alternatives, including a
  possible sale, ahead of the Amazon transaction.
- Real estate footprint skews toward dense, affluent urban markets — attractive
  as potential last-mile distribution/fulfillment nodes.

## Section 4: Risk Factors (Selected)

- Sustained comparable-sales pressure from lower-priced competitors expanding
  into natural/organic and private-label grocery.
- Premium pricing perception limiting the customer base in a category
  increasingly sensitive to price.
- A largely non-unionized, decentralized "team-based" store operating model
  that differs materially from a centralized logistics/technology operating
  culture.
- Perishable-goods supply chain complexity not present in most of an acquirer's
  existing distribution network.
